## FINDING 1 — Architecture, corrected: a tiered multi-agent router, not flat document-RAG

The visible planner trace is the most valuable artifact collected so far:

> "**Selected 3 data sources.** Direct request for two specific metrics on a specific date. **sleep_daily** is the only **agent** that contains deep sleep duration per night. **cardio_weekly** contains daily resting heart rate breakdowns (including night/day splits) and is the best available source for RHR on that date. No need for monthly or **records agents** for a single date query. **No Tier 2 agents** required as no trend, correlation, or anomaly analysis is requested."

Plus: `Executing 8 research queries…` and `Searching scientific literature…`

This is a **query router over named, tiered data agents** (`sleep_daily`, `cardio_weekly`, monthly agents, records agents; Tier 1 retrieval vs. Tier 2 trend/correlation/anomaly analysis), running alongside a **separate scientific-literature RAG**.

**My P1 conclusion was too crude and is hereby corrected.** "Pre-computed summary documents" understated it: there is genuine query planning and source selection. The *boundary* conclusion survives intact — Jade reads summary agents, not the raw timeseries, and its own words confirm it: **"I only have access to summarized daily, weekly, and monthly data."** That sentence is the single best citation in the study.

## FINDING 2 — The false-negative gap is a full year wide

Jade's stated coverage vs. the export:

| | Jade claims | Ring actually recorded |
|---|---|---|
| Earliest sleep data | **January 8, 2026** | **January 8, 2025** — 12 months earlier |
| Earliest cardio w/ daily RHR | March 2025 | January 8, 2025 |
| Most recent sleep | July 22–27, 2026 | (export ends Jul 23) |
| Most recent cardio | June 8 – August 3, 2026 | (export stale after Jul 23) |

The month-and-day match with a one-year offset (Jan 8 2026 vs Jan 8 2025) is too exact to be coincidence — it points to a **year-labelling error** in the summarisation layer, or a retention window that silently drops a year. Either way: **Jade is blind to roughly twelve months of sleep data the ring captured.** This is the P1 false-negative finding, at scale, self-reported.

## FINDING 3 — The real failure mode: a fabricated premise carrying real citations

Unprompted, Jade asserted:

> "Since you're **on a shift work schedule (typically 01:18–07:30)**, your sleep patterns may vary significantly… Shift work is associated with disrupted circadian rhythms and reduced sleep quality, including lower deep sleep percentages [2]."
> References: *Shift work and risk of sleep disturbances* (Springer); *Shift workers' experiences and views of sleep disturbance* (PubMed)

**The user is not a shift worker.** He is a graduate student with an irregular schedule.

Note precisely what did and did not go wrong:

| Layer | Status |
|---|---|
| The observed pattern (sleep ~01:18–07:30) | **Roughly correct** — my derived median is 02:30 → 07:10 |
| The inferred category ("shift work schedule") | **Fabricated** — a pattern converted into an occupational identity |
| The literature retrieved for that category | **Real, correctly cited** |
| The conclusion delivered to the user | **Wrong, and wearing citations** |

This is subtler and more dangerous than a wrong number. The numbers are right, the reasoning is coherent, the citations are genuine — and the output is health guidance for **a different person**. Confabulated *numbers* are catchable by anyone with an export. A confabulated *premise*, dressed in real peer-reviewed references, is not: it reads as the system's most rigorous output.

**This is now the case study's central finding.** It generalises past Ultrahuman: the industry's evaluation reflex is to check whether the AI's numbers are real, and Jade's are. Nobody checks whether the *person the AI is reasoning about* is real.
